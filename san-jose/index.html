Keeping Your San Jose Water Heater Running Longer

Routine maintenance makes all the difference between a water heater that lasts and one that floods the garage in year seven. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: an annual sediment flush, an anode rod check every three to five years, a yearly test of the T&P valve, a thermostat set near 120 degrees, and annual descaling for tankless units on hard water, so problems are found while they're still inexpensive.

How old is your water heater? The serial number on the label usually includes the month and year it was built, and its age says a lot about what comes next. Most tank models make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units around 20. If your heater is past that point and is also showing discolored water, rumbling, or moisture at the base, it's usually due for a planned replacement, and that's almost always cheaper and less stressful than a rushed emergency install once the tank fails.

The earlier you report a hot water problem, the cheaper it usually is. A tank that's just begun to rumble can often be fixed with a flush, yet that same buildup, ignored for a few years, can overheat the bottom of the tank and shorten the life of the tank, forcing a replacement much sooner. Save our number on your phone now so help is one call away the moment something seems off. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

Hot Water Headaches San Jose Homeowners Face

Most water heater problems we see in San Jose come back to the water itself. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be a real factor: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Equipment age plays a big role too. When the sacrificial anode rod is gone, often well before the tank itself looks old, the steel begins corroding from within, and a drip from the bottom seam is usually the first visible sign. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Setup matters too. A home on a private well can see more sediment and occasionally a sulfur smell in the hot water, often fixed by switching the anode rod type. A tankless heater in hard water needs regular descaling. And an older install being replaced often needs updates that weren't required when it went in: an expansion tank, a correctly piped relief valve line, a drain pan, or seismic straps where required. All of these can be fixed, and most are much cheaper when caught early. If something seems off, a diagnostic visit from our team can keep a small problem from turning into a flooded floor.
